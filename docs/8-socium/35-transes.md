
# Social Trances

## The Impostor

**Impostor pattern**: The felt tax of the Marketing Character. When worth is pinned to achievement, competence never feels owned — success is attributed to luck or timing, praise is discounted, and exposure as a "fraud" feels imminent.

- It is installed in childhood, when approval arrived conditional on performance, and persists as an adult reflex regardless of real skill.
- More achievement does not cure it; the pattern only re-discounts each new success. The exit is separating worth from output — the AspirationalSelf (Hero) is a direction to grow, not the price of being allowed to exist.

## The Inferiority Trance

Most people carry a quiet, near-constant sense that *something is wrong with me*. Tara Brach calls it the trance of unworthiness; here it is the `Inferiority Trance`. It is a trance in the literal sense: as in a dream you do not know you are dreaming, while inside it you do not know you are in it. It fills everything — thoughts, choices, relationships — yet stays invisible, the way a large dark shape can sit in plain view while attention fixes on a small bright one. It is close to universal. Even the arrogant who never admit a fault carry it; their armor usually hides a worse case. Its voice is the inner hypnotist: *not for someone like you; you should have stayed quiet; you let everyone down; you don't matter.* This extends [Acceptance of Imperfection](#acceptance-of-imperfection) and [Self-Honesty](#self-honesty), which name the same machinery from the side of the cure.

**Three sources install it.** None of them is escapable, so no one grows up uninfected.

- *Mass media.* Body and success ideals have inflated past what is naturally reachable — the modern standard for a man's body now requires drugs, not training, and the standard for women is harsher still. Chasing the image breeds `Body Dysmorphia`: obsessive distress over a small or imagined flaw, often paired with depression, `Anxiety`, and eating disorders. Surveys track the damage: dissatisfaction rises in lockstep with media exposure, and most reported shame about appearance falls on women. But media did not create the feeling; it *monetizes* it. The fitness, surgery, and supplement industries sell relief from a wound that was already there. We take the bait only because we already believed something was wrong with us.
- *Suppression of feeling.* The story of the expulsion from the garden is a metaphor for an old conflict: the `Animal` in us against the keeper of the social order. Our drives — aggression, lust, envy, hatred — can clash with the rules of a safe society, so morality brands them shameful. The error is that it brands not only harmful *acts* but the *feelings and thoughts* themselves. Behavior can sometimes be controlled; a feeling cannot, because it arrives unasked. Suppression is only pretense that the feeling is gone, and ignored feelings grow stronger, since a feeling is a signal of a need — a warning light on the dashboard that gets louder the longer it is ignored. Gendered rules ("girls don't get angry," "boys don't cry") add a second layer of suffering: shame about the feeling itself. A feeling owned and lived loses its charge; a feeling suppressed is an unexploded grenade — far more dangerous than one admitted. See [Self-Empathy and Self-Trust](#self-emphaty-and-self-trust).
- *Conditional love in childhood.* Carl Rogers split the self into the `Real Self` (who I feel I am) and the `Ideal Self` (who I want to be). Healthy growth needs unconditional acceptance — being valued regardless of success or error. When love is given only for performance ("good *if* you get top marks"), a gap opens: the `Real Self` feels defective and lagging, the `Ideal Self` rises into a punishing god that always finds you short. Worth and self-love stop being a resting state and become a prize that keeps receding. Five marks of this pattern: constant self-editing and tight self-control; fear of mistakes, because each one feels like proof of who you really are; no satisfaction even in success, since the bar moves with you; living inside others' expectations, cut off from your own needs and wants; and parents who pass it down — they were raised the same way, were never shown another option, and their own punishing `Ideal Self` simply switches its aim onto the child. Adults compound it by treating children as unfinished humans ("you'll have opinions when you grow up"), so the child learns to feel unreal and unimportant, hoping worth will arrive with adulthood. It does not arrive on its own.

**It hides, but it can be located by how you orbit it.** The trance is unconscious by definition, but an invisible weight bends every path around it — your standing habits point straight at it. There are three strategies for coping with it, and most people run a mix.

- *Avoidance.* Two forms. Denial — *this isn't about me, it's about other people* — where shame is so shameful we are ashamed even to feel it. And distraction through addiction: alcohol, gaming, food, risk, self-harm, even "self-improvement" run as a permitted way to stay too busy to feel the pain. Distraction breeds a vicious circle (ashamed → numb the shame → ashamed of that → numb again). Self-harm often wears socially approved disguises — over-training, extreme diets, cosmetic procedures used as punishment or as a way to "earn" brief relief from the shame.
- *Fighting.* Offload your inferiority onto others. Shaming someone lowers them so you feel less worthless by contrast — and the trance is contagious, so it spreads. Narcissism is the extreme: not self-love but self-hatred projected outward. "Warriors" who attack the opposite sex are usually people who were hurt and now attack first to avoid feeling the wound again. The person in the spotless white coat who is never wrong is defending the dam that holds back the pain — and the defense backfires, because hiding every failure feeds the fear of failure and roots it deeper. Hostility toward the world is proportional to one's own `Inferiority Trance`.
- *Compensation.* Two sub-strategies. *Be pleasing* — accommodate everyone, drop your boundaries, earn good treatment because you assume you don't deserve it freely. *Be great* — become so large that the stigma shrinks to nothing against your achievements. This is the engine of Gatsby: wealth, status, and the girl are all attempts to outgrow a childhood sense of inferiority, and none of them fill the hole. External attributes never grant worth: about 80% of people with `Body Dysmorphia` remain dissatisfied after cosmetic surgery and only ~1% are actually helped, because the problem was never the body but the perception of self — which the procedure leaves untouched.

**The same split runs your motivation.** Avoidance motivation (also called away-from) is driven by disgust and fear of where you are: *I'm ashamed of being weak, so I must fix myself.* It is explosive but short-lived — over 60% of new gym members quit within six months — and it turns effort into self-punishment, so it breaks people. Aspiration motivation (toward) moves the focus from the place you flee to the place and the doing you enjoy. It burns lower but lasts: real achievement is a marathon, run by pacing yourself and so by accepting your limits, and where failure is not catastrophe but information. Acting from love, you improve your body or your work because you care for it, not because you hate it. The deeper power of love is not that it climbs to the goal more efficiently — it is that you are already alright *without* the goal. This is the basis of [Confidence](#confidence) and connects to [Love](../4-human/23-rajdo.md).

**The way out is not fixing it.** Fighting, fleeing, or repairing the trance all act from the premise *something is wrong with me*, so each attempt re-confirms it — fighting shame summons more shame, like dousing a fire with fuel. The exit is the opposite move: `Radical Acceptance` (Tara Brach), reduced here to three steps.

- *Recognize.* To wake from a bad dream you first see that you are dreaming. Catch the trance by its tells — the merciless inner voice, fear, shame, envy, `Anxiety` — and name the feeling plainly, aloud: "I feel shame." Naming is not a trick: putting a feeling into words lowers amygdala activity and raises prefrontal activity, which weakens the emotional reaction and breaks the automatic fight/flee/fix response.
- *Allow.* Let it be. Acceptance is not approval and not the hunt for a silver lining; it is neutral, simply the absence of resistance — the way you accept that today it rains. Let the feeling register in the body — the tight throat, the cold weight in the chest — without papering over it. Once the friction between you and the feeling stops, the friction that was striking the suffering stops with it.
- *Be compassionate.* The hardest step. We exiled ourselves: the world once wanted something other than what you were, and now you do the same to your own trembling and vulnerability. So nothing needs to be *done* to the inferiority — trying to smooth it is like smoothing ripples on water, where every touch makes more. Leave it as it is, not from surrender but from the unconditional acceptance and `Self-Compassion` the world never gave you. If you can feel compassion for others, you can turn it on yourself. The aim is to become, for yourself, the kind of presence that quiets the inferiority voice in others — not by flattery, which only deepens the shame, but by taking the person seriously and being willing to see them as they are. Give yourself permission to be weak, to fail, to look foolish, to achieve nothing, and to be loved anyway. See [Self-Empathy and Self-Trust](#self-emphaty-and-self-trust) and [Acceptance of Imperfection](#acceptance-of-imperfection).

The honest treatment for an entrenched `Inferiority Trance` is psychotherapy — we call a plumber for a broken pipe but try to be both the electrician and the burnt-out bulb for the far more delicate wiring of the mind. The three steps above are the daily practice that makes the work hold.

## Conformism and Moral Courage

`Conformism` is not a neutral position. It is an active choice with a cost — to the person and to everyone around them. `Moral Courage` is its opposite: acting on your own judgment against social pressure.

**The default is obedience.** In Milgram's 1963 experiment, ordinary people were told to deliver rising electric shocks to a stranger and could hear the cries through the wall. 65% went to the maximum, the dial marked *death*. They were not sadists; they obeyed because a calm man in a white coat said "continue, the experiment requires it." The lesson is not that most people are cruel, but that most people comply.

**Modern `Conformism` wears a good face.** It is not the obvious coward who runs or betrays. It is the decent person who believes that being good means not making trouble, not rocking the boat, doing what is expected. Rollo May (1953) named this *automatic conformity* and called it the opposite of courage — not trembling and hiding, but functioning like a well-tuned mechanism that never asks a question.

**The conformist defends the system.** He cannot be neutral. Another person's independence is a threat: if one can think, speak, and live differently, then why did he stay silent, agree, and endure? To protect his own choice of comfort, he becomes the first to point at the rule-breaker and the first to support restrictions on those who do not fit. Stanley Feldman documented this: people who prize social conformity actively support the state when it tightens control and punishes those who break norms.

**Where it is manufactured.** Conformism is produced, not innate. School rewards the one right answer and corrects the child who doubts the authority. Social media adds real-time, measured pressure — likes and shares reward agreement with the current majority and punish independence — and it turned every opinion into a public act, making self-censorship a rational survival strategy. The workplace reads dissent as disloyalty. Each environment teaches the same lesson: keep your real view to yourself.

**Small steps go far.** Ervin Staub studied how ordinary people in the Soviet Union, Germany, Cambodia, and China became accomplices to mass killing. He called it the *continuum of destruction*. It never starts with an order to kill. It starts with a small step — one group barred from a café, then restricted, then fired, then fined. Each step is small, justified by safety or order, and most stay silent because the step is small and "it does not touch me." But silence is consent, and each next step is easier because the last one met no resistance. To live with the harm, people devalue the victim — "they brought it on themselves." This is not the psychology of a villain; it is the psychology of an ordinary person who made one small choice to stay silent and must now defend it.

**Courage is action despite fear.** `Moral Courage` is not the absence of [Fear](../4-human/28-animal.md); it is acting against fear because something matters more than comfort. It rarely looks like the movies — no speech, no applause. It begins in small daily moments: not laughing at a joke you find vile, not agreeing with a claim you believe is false just because arguing is awkward, not endorsing what you think is unjust just because the majority does. Each small act is training. A muscle that is not used atrophies; the capacity to hold a position works the same way. See [Courage and Enjoyment](#courage-and-enjoyment).

**Two reasons the small acts matter.** Inward: every time you stay silent where you should have spoken, you pay a small cost in self-respect; the payments are invisible alone but compound, until you no longer know what you yourself think. This is the slavery Goethe meant — "He who cannot be master of himself will always be a slave." Outward: conformism runs on the appearance of unanimity. The moment one person says aloud what many think privately, the illusion breaks.

**One voice changes the room.** This is measured, not metaphor. In Asch's 1951 line experiment, 75% conformed to an obviously wrong majority at least once; a single dissenting voice cut conformity from 75% to about 5%. In Milgram's setup, one person who refused dropped maximum obedience from 65% to 10%. Psychologists call the trap *pluralistic ignorance* — each person privately disagrees but fakes agreement because everyone else seems to, so most stay silent believing they are the minority when they are not. A consistent minority can shift the majority — but only if it speaks.

**Practice — four moves:**

- *Close the gap between private and public view.* Thinking one thing and saying another is not diplomacy; it is slow self-erosion. The size of that gap measures your `Conformism`. See [Self-Honesty](#self-honesty).
- *Separate morality from law.* Permitted does not mean right; forbidden does not mean wrong. Obedience by itself is not a virtue — a well-trained dog obeys perfectly.
- *Take small risks regularly.* Do not wait for a heroic moment. Practice dissent in small things so the capacity stays alive.
- *Stop waiting for someone else to speak first.* The bystander effect applies to honesty: everyone defers to a more qualified someone, and that someone thinks the same of you, so no one speaks.

**Responsibility is the dividing line.** Milgram found the predictor of refusal was not intelligence or politics but the willingness to own one's actions. Those who refused said "I am responsible for this"; those who continued said "I am only following instructions." Delegating responsibility is the heart of conformism — its antidote is [Accountability](#accountability). Viktor Pestov understood this at twenty: after Soviet tanks crushed the Prague Spring in 1968, he formed an underground group, warned each member they would likely be arrested within a year, and printed and posted leaflets anyway. He got five years in a labor camp; his mother lost her KGB post the same day. Asked later, he said a person must be master of his own fate, because if you do not choose, someone else chooses for you. Solzhenitsyn, after eight years in the camps, reduced the standard to a minimum that needs no heroism: *do not live by lies* — do not support a lie with your silence, do not applaud what you think is wrong, do not say what you do not think.

The Stoics treated this as practical, not abstract. Marcus Aurelius: separate what is in your power from what is not, spend effort only on the former, accept the rest — how you are received is never yours to control, so do not spend yourself on it. Epictetus, who was a slave, saw that outer freedom can be taken but inner freedom cannot: no one can make you believe what you do not believe unless you hand it over. The conformist hands it over voluntarily and gradually, because *self-perception* runs both ways — behave like a coward and you come to see yourself as one; act with a position and that becomes your identity.

Milgram's number is usually cited as 65% obeyed. The number cited less often: 35% refused — ordinary people, no special training, who at some point said "no, I will not do this." The choice is always there.
